#02 Case study

Channel Partner Project

From fragmented partner workflows to a connected customer lifecycle. An end-to-end product design project spanning research, workflows, information architecture, interface design and launch for Channel Partners, MIS teams and managers.

In short
Problem
Manual, fragmented CP workflows — no one had a clear view of lead approvals, follow-ups or commission status.
Approach
End-to-end, research-led product design that connected the customer lifecycle around one shared customer record.
Outcome
A digitised, transparent workflow — visible status, structured approvals and automated reporting for CPs, MIS and managers.

02 — The problem

A fragmented, largely manual process.

Customer information, approvals, follow-ups and commission updates were spread across different people and processes, most of them physical and manual.

CPs couldn't easily understand where their customers stood, while MIS teams and managers lacked a consistent, transparent way to manage approvals, follow-ups and reporting.
01

Follow-up visibility

Customer follow-ups were difficult to track across MIS, CPs and customers.

02

Approval visibility

CPs had limited visibility into CP and lead approval status.

03

Customer visibility

CPs couldn't easily see the complete status of each customer.

04

Commission visibility

Commission progress was not easily accessible.

05

Process delays

Manual handoffs could create unexpected delays.

06

Reporting

Teams needed easier access to recurring operational and performance reports.

03 — Why this wasn't a UI problem

The underlying problem was the workflow itself.

A CP needed answers to questions that no single screen could give them — because the product treated each moment as an isolated module instead of connecting them.

Has my lead been approved?
What is my customer's current status?
When should I follow up?
Did the customer visit the site?
Did they submit an EOI?
Did they book?
What is my commission status?

05 — Research

Understanding the process before shaping the end-to-end experience.

Research question: where does information get lost, delayed, or become difficult to understand?

Research activities

  • User-role analysis
  • Competitor research
  • Customer journey analysis
  • Approval & follow-up flow analysis
  • Commission workflow analysis
  • Reporting requirements

Key findings

01 · Follow-up

Unclear status & history

CPs needed a clearer way to understand customer follow-up status and history.

02 · CP approval

Limited visibility

The approval process lacked sufficient visibility and could create delays.

03 · Lead approval

Ambiguous state

CPs needed clearer visibility into whether a lead was pending, approved or rejected.

Users didn't simply need more information. They needed the right information at the right stage of the workflow.

06 — Strategy

From module-based thinking to lifecycle-based thinking.

Design principle: every important screen should help the user understand what happened, what is happening, and what needs to happen next.

08 — Key UX decisions

Where the workflow thinking became interface thinking.

Decision 01

Customer 360

Instead of forcing users through multiple modules, the customer record connects every stage of the journey in one place — the goal: give CPs a single place to understand where their customer stands.

Customer profile→ Lead info→ CP info→ Approval→ Follow-up→ Site visit→ EOI→ Booking→ Commission
Decision 02

The multiple-CP challenge

A single customer is sometimes connected to more than one Channel Partner — creating risk around duplicate leads, CP ownership, customer association, approval conflicts and data consistency. The solution had to balance business control with clear user feedback.

MIS Force Approval gives MIS the final operational call while keeping the CP–customer relationship controlled and traceable.

Potential conflict↓ MIS reviews customer + CP info↓ MIS force-approves↓ CP association confirmed↓ CP notified
Decision 03

Approval experience

Designed around speed and transparency, with a target of roughly a two-minute MIS approval turnaround.

CP submits lead→ MIS reviews→ Approve / reject / force-approve→ CP notified (in-app + email)→ Customer status updates

Note: the ~2-minute figure is a design target, not measured production data.

Decision 04

Follow-up, made visible by default

Every customer carries a current status, follow-up date, follow-up history, next action and stage — so nobody has to search for what to do next.

Don't make users search for the next action. Make it visible.
Decision 05

Dashboard: from reporting to action

The dashboard answers one question first — what should I work on today? — through a dedicated "needs attention" view alongside the customer overview.

Overview

  • Total customers
  • Active leads
  • Pending approvals
  • Follow-ups / visits / EOIs
  • Booking & commission status

Needs attention

  • Pending lead approvals
  • Today's follow-ups
  • Overdue follow-ups
  • Upcoming site visits
  • Pending commission actions
Decision 06

Commission, as a visible journey

Commission tracking was redesigned as a clear status lifecycle rather than a single opaque line item.

Eligible→ Claim submitted→ Under review→ Approved→ Payment processing→ Paid
Decision 07

Notifications & automated reporting

Important workflow events — like a lead approval — trigger both an in-app and an email notification, closing the loop without manual status chasing. Daily, weekly and monthly reports are available on the dashboard and delivered automatically by email to CPs, MIS and managers.

MIS action→ System update→ CP notification→ CP takes next action

12 — What I learned

Five things this project reinforced.

01

Enterprise UX is workflow design

The hardest problems weren't visual — they were about relationships, ownership, approvals and business rules.

02

Visibility is a product feature

Showing information isn't enough. Users need status, context and next action together.

03

Different roles, different mental models

A CP, an MIS executive and a manager look at the same customer record with very different goals.

04

Design doesn't end in Figma

Dev collaboration, site implementation, validation and user support were as much a part of this as the screens.

05

Real-world constraints shape good UX

The multiple-CP scenario shows how a business rule can directly drive information architecture and interaction design.

Product philosophy

"Make every customer status visible. Make every next action clear."

The project evolved from a collection of disconnected modules into a connected customer-lifecycle platform.

Lead→Approval→ Follow-up→Site visit→ EOI→Booking→Commission

Supported throughout by notifications, the dashboard, and automated reporting.

This was a complete CP platform project, not a single-screen redesign: I carried the work from discovery and workflow design through implementation, launch and user support.
